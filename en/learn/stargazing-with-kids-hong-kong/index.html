Stargazing with kids in Hong Kong:
start with the moon

You can stargaze with your kids even in light-polluted Hong Kong. Start with the moon's phases, bright stars and planets, then choose equipment, places, seasonal targets and safety, with a free moon log.

What shall we do today?

Many parents think Hong Kong's light pollution means stargazing requires a trip far into the countryside. In fact, the moon, bright planets and the brightest stars are visible from a rooftop or podium in the city. Start simple, and stargazing can become a 10-minute family ritual every evening.

Step 1: just your eyes, and the moon

The moon is the best place to start. You don't need any equipment, and children can find it most nights (except around the new moon). Record its shape for two to four weeks and your child will discover an important pattern: the phases repeat roughly every 29.5 days.

Download the 30-day moon phase log, stick it on the fridge and draw the moon every night.

PhaseRoughly whenViewing tips
New moon1st day of the lunar monthThe moon is almost invisible, the best time for stars
First quarterAround the 7th–8th lunar dayAlready up in the early evening, perfect for children
Full moonAround the 15th lunar dayVisible all night, but very bright, so craters look flatter
Last quarterAround the 22nd–23rd lunar dayRises after midnight, better for early risers

Step 2: find the brightest “stars”

In the city, you can usually only see the brightest objects with the naked eye. Some of them are actually planets: they're usually brighter than nearby stars and shine more steadily, with less twinkling. Venus often appears low in the sky at dusk or dawn, while Jupiter and Saturn appear in different seasons.

Winter evenings are a great time to learn constellations: the three stars of Orion's Belt in a straight line are easy to spot even from urban Hong Kong.

Planet positions change every year. Check the sky information published by the Hong Kong Observatory or Hong Kong Space Museum.

BinocularsAge 5Light and dark areas on the moon, more stars
TelescopeAges 6–7 (with an adult)Moon craters, Jupiter's moons, Saturn's rings

Where to stargaze

  • Your rooftop or podium: enough for the moon and planets, and the easiest way to build a habit.
  • Open waterfronts or parks: a wider view, so you can see planets low in the sky.
  • The countryside: for more stars, go somewhere with less light pollution, such as the High Island Reservoir area in Sai Kung, home to the Hong Kong Astropark. Check the weather and transport before you go.

Before you go: a checklist

  • Check the weather: clear nights with little cloud are best.
  • Let your eyes adjust to the dark for 10–15 minutes, avoiding bright phone screens.
  • Bring a jacket and insect repellent. Nights in the countryside are cooler.
  • Bring a red torch (or wrap a torch in red cellophane) so you keep your night vision.
  • Bring a stargazing log card and a pencil.
⚠️ Safety: never stare at the sun with the naked eye, and never look at it through a telescope or binoculars. To watch events like solar eclipses, use a certified solar filter, with an adult supervising the whole time.

FAQ

Can you really stargaze in the city?

Yes. The moon and bright planets are barely affected by light pollution, and with binoculars or a telescope you can see plenty of detail from a rooftop.

My child goes to bed early. How can we stargaze?

Choose the days around the first quarter, when the moon is already up in the early evening, so you can observe for 10 minutes after dinner.

Are there stargazing apps for kids?

Sky map apps can help identify constellations and planets, but dim the screen and focus on looking with your eyes. The Kidrise telescope also has a companion Star Explorer web app with stargazing missions.
