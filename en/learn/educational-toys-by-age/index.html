What shall we do today?

“Is this toy right for my child?” is the question parents ask most. Every child develops at their own pace, but knowing roughly what matters at each stage helps you avoid toys that are too easy or too hard. Here's a guide to five age groups for shopping or gift-giving.

Three rules for choosing toys: ① the right age matters more than “advanced”; ② it can be played in more than one way; ③ a grown-up plays too.

Ages 1–2: discovering the world through the senses

Developing: grasping, banging, putting in and taking out; understanding cause and effect (“I do this, and that happens”); first simple words.

Good toys: cloth books, xylophones, stacking cups, shape sorters, posting toys, large blocks.

How to play: describe as you go (“The red cup goes in!”), repeat a lot, and let your child lead.

Safety: avoid small parts and long cords. Toys should stand up to chewing and be washable.

Ages 3–4: nimbler hands, first rules

Developing: fine motor control (ready for holding a pencil), matching colours and shapes, counting, and social skills like pretend play and taking turns.

Good toys: magnetic bead mazes, screwing toys, matching games, play kitchens, simple cooperative board games, jigsaws (9–24 pieces).

How to play: in pretend play, let your child be the shopkeeper or doctor and follow their story. Start with cooperative games to avoid upsets about winning and losing.

Ages 5–6: a burst of curiosity and endless “why?”

Developing: logical reasoning, simple adding and subtracting, predicting and checking, and attention spans of 15–20 minutes.

Good toys: colour code games, magnetic tiles, binoculars, simple science experiments, and balance games like stacking towers.

How to play: turn each “why?” into a mini experiment: “Shall we test it?” Try our free family activities.

Ages 7–8: hands-on experiments and models

Developing: in primary school, children meet scientific inquiry. They can follow steps to build a model and understand simple mechanisms.

Good toys: kids' microscopes, telescopes, STEM wooden models, maths strategy games, magnetic puzzles.

How to play: encourage recording: drawing, taking photos or writing three observations turns “playing” into “research”.

Ages 9–12: complete projects

Developing: abstract thinking, planning and independent problem solving. They need challenging goals that bring a sense of achievement.

Good toys: hydraulic or solar mechanical kits, coding robots, circuit experiments, digital microscopes, advanced strategy games.

How to play: let your child set their own goals and timeline, with you as an adviser rather than a teacher. When they finish, encourage them to present the result to the family.

FAQ

Do I have to follow the age label on the box exactly?

Always follow “not suitable for under 3” warnings, which are usually about small parts. For the upper end, a capable child can start a little early, as long as the toy won't leave them frustrated.

My child loses interest after a few days. What can I do?

Try toy rotation: put some toys away and bring them back out after 1–2 weeks. Download our toy rotation planner.

What should I buy for siblings with a big age gap?

Open-ended toys (blocks, magnetic tiles) and cooperative board games work best. The older child can take on harder challenges while the younger one plays a simpler version.
