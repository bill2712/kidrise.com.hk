Fine motor skills:
12 games for ages 1–6

Fine motor skills shape how children hold a pencil, use scissors, do up buttons and look after themselves. Milestones for ages 1–6, 12 games to play at home and when to seek advice.

What shall we do today?

Fine motor skills use the small muscles of the fingers, hands and wrists for precise movements like pinching, squeezing, twisting, cutting and holding a pencil. When these skills develop well, writing is easier when children start primary school, and they're more confident with self-care like buttons, zips and cutlery.

In short: fine motor skills grow through daily play, not daily writing. Pinching, rolling, tearing, twisting and cutting build a better foundation for holding a pencil than lots of early writing.

12 games to build fine motor skills at home

1. Clothes peg challenge

Clip pegs around a paper plate to build fingertip strength. (Activity card)

2. Torn paper collage

Tear old magazines into pieces and stick them into a picture to practise using both hands.

3. Play dough balls

Rolling, pressing, pinching and cutting make the perfect hand warm-up.

4. Bean transfer

Move beans from one bowl to another with a spoon or tongs.

5. Pasta necklace

Thread pasta tubes onto a shoelace to practise aiming and hand–eye coordination.

6. Lids on, lids off

Set out bottles of different sizes to match lids and screw them on tight.

7. Sticker lines

Stick stickers along a line to practise peeling and placing.

8. Scissor practice

Start with straight lines, then zigzags, waves and spirals. (Practice sheet)

9. Dropper painting

Drip coloured water onto kitchen paper to practise controlled squeezing.

10. Button practice

Start with big buttons and play “dress the teddy”.

11. Mazes

Finger mazes and magnetic bead mazes train a steady wrist and pencil direction.

12. Screw-together kits

Turning screws needs wrist rotation and controlled force, and it's a first taste of engineering.

Getting ready to hold a pencil

  • Posture: feet flat on the floor, table at about elbow height.
  • Shorter is better: short crayons or chunky triangular pencils naturally encourage a three-finger grip.
  • Draw on a vertical surface: paper on a wall or an easel helps keep the wrist straight and the shoulder stable.
  • 10 minutes at a time: young hands tire easily, so short, frequent sessions work best.

When should I seek professional advice?

If your child finds everyday fine motor tasks much harder than other children their age (for example, still struggling to hold a spoon or crayon steadily after age 4), often avoids activities that use their hands, or a teacher raises concerns, start by asking your family doctor or a Maternal and Child Health Centre. They can refer you to an occupational therapist for assessment if needed. Early support works best.

FAQ

My 3-year-old's pencil grip isn't right. Should I correct it straight away?

Grips vary a lot at age 3 and that's normal. Rather than constantly correcting, play more fine motor games and offer short or chunky pencils so fingers find a good grip naturally.

When can children start using scissors?

From about age 3, with an adult and round-tipped children's scissors. Start with single snips across a paper strip, then move on to straight lines and curves.

Do drawing games on a tablet build fine motor skills?

Only a little. Swiping on a screen lacks resistance and texture. Real paper and pencils, play dough and pegs do much more for hand muscles.
