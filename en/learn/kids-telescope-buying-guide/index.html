Choosing a kids' telescope:
a parent's starter guide

The most important thing in a kids' telescope is aperture, not magnification. A plain-English guide to aperture, magnification, refractors vs reflectors, mounts, what you can see from Hong Kong and first steps.

What shall we do today?

Many parents buying their first telescope are drawn to numbers like “675x” or “1000x” on the box. But what really decides how clear the view is are the aperture and a steady mount. This guide explains, as simply as possible, how to choose a starter telescope your 6–12-year-old will really use.

In short: for primary school beginners, a 60–80mm refractor on a steady alt-azimuth mount with a low-power eyepiece is enough to see the moon's craters, Jupiter's moons and Saturn's rings clearly.

Aperture: a telescope's most important spec

Aperture is the diameter of the telescope's main lens or mirror, usually in millimetres (mm). The bigger the aperture, the more light it collects, so the image is brighter and sharper and shows more detail.

ApertureSuitsWhat you can see
Under 50mmA first taste for young childrenThe moon's outline, bright planets as dots of light
60–80mmThe best start for ages 6–12Moon craters, Jupiter and its moons, Saturn's rings, the phases of Venus
100mm+Upper primary to adult enthusiastsMore planetary detail, brighter star clusters and nebulae (with darker skies)

The magnification myth: higher isn't better

A telescope's magnification can actually be changed at will. It's calculated as:

Magnification = telescope focal length ÷ eyepiece focal length

So a telescope with a 700mm focal length and a 20mm eyepiece gives 35x; with a 10mm eyepiece it's 70x. As a rule, the useful maximum magnification is about twice the aperture in mm, so around 120x is the limit for a 60mm telescope. Beyond that, the image is dim and blurry and shakes at the slightest touch, and children quickly lose interest.

What parents should check is whether the telescope comes with a low-power eyepiece (such as 20mm or 25mm). A low-power view is wide and makes targets easy to find, which is the key to a successful first night.

Refractor or reflector?

Refractor (lenses)Reflector (mirrors)
DesignSealed tube, almost no maintenanceOpen tube, needs alignment now and then
ImageUpright (with a diagonal), good for daytime views tooCheaper for the same aperture, can go bigger
WeightLight and easy to take outHeavier at larger apertures
SuitsChildren and beginnersExperienced users who want fainter objects

The mount: the most overlooked part

Even the best tube won't give a clear view on a wobbly mount. For beginners we recommend an alt-azimuth mount (moves up, down, left and right). It's intuitive and children pick it up quickly. An equatorial mount can track the stars as they move, but it's more complicated to set up, so it suits upper-primary children with a deeper interest.

When choosing, check whether the height adjusts (so your child can look comfortably standing or sitting), whether it wobbles once locked, and whether it has slow-motion controls.

What can you see from Hong Kong?

Hong Kong has a lot of light pollution, but it barely affects the moon and planets. From a rooftop, podium or window at home you can already see:

  • The moon: the best first target. Around the first quarter, the craters along the line between light and dark look the most 3D.
  • Jupiter: a small disc with a few moons in a line beside it, which change position every night.
  • Saturn: at the right magnification you can see the rings, often children's most amazing moment.
  • Venus: shows phases, just like the moon.

To see more stars, head to darker countryside such as the High Island Reservoir area in Sai Kung, home to the Hong Kong Astropark. Check the Hong Kong Observatory's weather and astronomy information before you go.

Your first night of stargazing: 5 steps

  1. Practise in daylight: aim the low-power eyepiece at a distant building or hilltop and align the finder scope at the same time.
  2. Let the telescope adjust: after taking it outside, wait 15–20 minutes for a steadier image.
  3. Start with the moon: it's big and easy to find, so children succeed quickly.
  4. Always start at low power: centre the target, then switch to a higher-power eyepiece.
  5. Keep a record: draw what you see on a stargazing log card to compare next time.
⚠️ Safety first: never point a telescope, binoculars or finder scope at the sun, even for a second. It can cause permanent eye damage. An adult must supervise any daytime use.

What about binoculars?

For children aged 4–7, light kids' binoculars are a great start: for birds, the moon and views on walks, and simpler to use than a telescope. Once your child is hooked on the night sky, there's plenty of time to upgrade.

FAQ

What age is right for a telescope?

Generally from age 6, when a child can close one eye and focus through the eyepiece, though they'll need an adult to aim it. From about 8, children can usually work an alt-azimuth mount themselves.

Can I photograph the moon with my phone?

Yes. Use a phone holder to line the camera up with the eyepiece. The moon is the easiest target, and children love sticking their photos into an observation diary.

Can we look through a closed window?

You can see the moon, but the glass blurs the image and causes reflections. If possible, open the window or go outside for a much better view.

Does a telescope need maintenance?

Put the lens cap on after use, store it somewhere dry and avoid touching the lens. Hong Kong is humid, so add a desiccant pack to the storage box.
