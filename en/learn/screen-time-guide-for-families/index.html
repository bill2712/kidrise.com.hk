Screen time for kids:
practical advice for Hong Kong families

How long should children use screens each day? Key points from WHO and Hong Kong Department of Health advice, plus how to set a family screen agreement, reduce arguments and find alternatives.

Looking for more family activity ideas?

What shall we do today?

Tablets, phones and TV are part of life, and banning them completely isn't realistic. What parents really need is a clear, consistent set of rules the whole family can follow, and offline options that are appealing enough.

In short: the younger the child, the less screen time the better. It's not just “how long”, but also “what”, “with whom”, and whether it affects sleep, exercise and family time.

Key points from official advice

AgeKey advice (summary)
Under 2Avoid screens (except video calls with family).
2–5No more than 1 hour a day, less is better, watching with an adult and choosing quality content.
6–12Set a schedule together, with a clear limit on entertainment use, protecting time for sleep, exercise and homework.

Based on the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines on physical activity and sleep for children under 5 and related health advice from Hong Kong's Department of Health. Please refer to the latest official information for details.

4 questions that matter more than the timer

What are they watching?

Slow-paced content with a story and interaction suits young children better than fast-cut short videos.

Who are they watching with?

Watching and talking about it with an adult turns screen time into language and thinking practice.

When are they watching?

Avoid screens in the hour before bed. Blue light and exciting content both affect sleep.

What is it replacing?

If screens are replacing outdoor play, sleep or family meals, it's time to adjust.

5 steps to a family screen agreement

  1. Talk it over as a family: children follow rules they helped make.
  2. Be clear on when, how long and where: for example, “after homework, 30 minutes a day, only in the living room”.
  3. Give a warning before time's up: a 5-minute reminder or a timer reduces upsets from sudden interruptions.
  4. Lead by example: no phones at the table or in bedrooms applies to adults too.
  5. Write it down and sign it: use the family screen time agreement, put it somewhere visible and review it monthly.

What now the screen is off? Make an “offline menu”

Many arguments start with “there's nothing to do” once the screen is off. List 10 screen-free activities with your child in advance and stick the list on the fridge so they can choose:

  • A 10-minute board game or jigsaw
  • A paper cup tower challenge (activity card)
  • Storytelling with story dice (activity card)
  • A home treasure map (activity card)
  • Nature bingo in the park downstairs

Meltdowns when the screen goes off?

This is a very common reaction: screen content is designed to be compelling, and stopping suddenly feels like a loss. Acknowledge the feeling (“I know you really want to keep watching”), hold the rule (“but time's up”) and offer the next choice (“Do you want to build with magnetic tiles or play a board game?”). Keep the rules consistent and arguments usually drop noticeably within a few weeks.

FAQ

Do educational apps count as screen time?

We suggest counting them, but you can separate learning use from entertainment. Even with educational content, watch posture, breaks and viewing distance.

Can we relax the rules at weekends?

You can agree special weekend plans in advance, like a family movie. The key is to agree the rules beforehand rather than negotiating on the spot.

What about their eyes?

Keep a suitable distance, take a break every 20 to 30 minutes to look into the distance, and make sure there's enough outdoor time every day. If you have concerns about vision, consult an eye care professional.
