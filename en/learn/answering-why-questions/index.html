Endless “why?” questions?
5 ways to answer

“Why is the sky blue?” “Why does the moon follow me?” Children's questions are where science begins. Five ways to respond, what to do when you don't know, and how to turn questions into experiments.

Looking for more family activity ideas?

What shall we do today?

Children aged 3 to 6 can ask “why?” dozens of times a day. When you're tired it's tempting to say “just because”, but every question is a sign your child wants to understand the world, and the best chance to build scientific thinking.

In short: you don't have to answer every question perfectly. Rather than handing over the answer, thinking, testing and looking it up together keeps your child's curiosity alive.

5 ways to answer “why?”

1. Ask back: “What do you think?”

Hear your child's idea first. Their answers are often wildly imaginative, and that is exactly how they practise making a hypothesis. Right or wrong, you can say: “What an interesting idea. How could we find out if it's true?”

2. Observe together

“Why do shadows get longer?” Measure a shadow at 3pm and again at 5pm today. When a question can be observed, let your child see the answer with their own eyes.

3. Try a mini experiment

“Why do some things float?” Fill a tub with water and test things from around the house. (See the Sink or Float activity.)

4. Look it up together

If you don't know, it's fine to say “I don't know either, let's find out together.” Use a children's encyclopedia, the library or a reliable website, so your child learns how to find answers.

5. Write it down for later

When you're busy, try: “That's a great question. Let's write it down and investigate tonight.” Recording it in a “Why?” question journal shows your child their questions matter.

Turning common “whys” into experiments

Your child asksHow you can explore it
Why does the moon change shape?Draw the moon every night for a month (moon phase log)
Why does my shadow follow me?Shine a torch on a toy from different angles (Shadow Zoo)
Why does ice melt?Compare ice cubes left indoors, by the window and in the fridge
How do ants find food?Leave a small biscuit crumb in the park and watch the ants' route
Why are leaves green?Look at leaves with a magnifier and microscope (The World Under a Magnifier)

3 things to avoid saying

  • “Just because.” Your child learns that asking questions is pointless.
  • “You'll understand when you're older.” You miss a chance to explore together.
  • “Not again!” Your child learns that curiosity is a nuisance.

If you really are exhausted, be honest: “I'm very tired right now. Shall we talk about it at breakfast tomorrow?” Then remember to keep your promise.

FAQ

What if my child asks a science question that's really hard to explain?

Simplify it with a comparison they understand, such as “light travels like waves” to explain colour, and admit that “scientists have studied this for a long time”. The goal is to spark interest, not to explain everything at once.

How should I answer sensitive questions, such as about life and death?

Answer honestly, briefly and in an age-appropriate way, and watch how your child is feeling. If they seem upset, spend more time listening and being with them first.

My child asks fewer questions as they get older. Is that normal?

Very common. Doing experiments together, watching documentaries and visiting museums can rekindle curiosity, and so can making your home a place where asking questions is a good thing.
