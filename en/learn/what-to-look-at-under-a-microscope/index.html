What to look at under a microscope:
20 samples from home

Bought a kids' microscope but not sure what to look at? 20 samples from your home, kitchen and park, how to make simple slides, how to focus and how to record, so the microscope doesn't end up in a cupboard.

What shall we do today?

Many families buy a microscope, look at the included slides and then don't know what to do next. In fact, everyday things at home become another world under a microscope: salt is tiny cubes, hair has scales, and leaves have tiny pores. These 20 samples are enough for a whole term.

Starter rules: samples should be thin, flat and well lit. Find your target at the lowest magnification first, then increase slowly.

Making a simple slide in 4 steps

  1. Add the sample: put a little in the middle of the slide (the thinner the better).
  2. Add a drop of water: for wet samples like onion skin or leaves.
  3. Add the cover slip: lower it at an angle from one side to reduce bubbles.
  4. Soak up extra water: dab the edge gently with the corner of a tissue.

To make onion cells clearer, an adult can help add a drop of diluted iodine to stain them, which makes the nuclei stand out. Iodine must not be swallowed; wash hands afterwards.

Focusing without cracking the slide

  1. Start with the lowest objective and turn on the light.
  2. Looking from the side, lower the objective close to the slide (without touching it).
  3. Looking through the eyepiece, slowly raise the objective until the image is sharp.
  4. Move the target to the centre of the view before switching to higher power, then use fine focus.

Recording: turn observations into science notes

Ask your child to draw what they see each time, with the sample name, magnification and three describing words. Use the microscope observation log, and in a year they'll have their own “micro field guide”. With a digital microscope, you can photograph each view and stick it beside the drawing to compare.

FAQ

Why do I only see white light or darkness?

Usually the sample is too thick, the light is too low, or it's out of focus. Start at the lowest magnification, check the sample is in the centre of the light, then focus slowly.

Can we see bacteria?

Bacteria are extremely small and usually need high magnification, oil immersion and staining, so they're hard to see clearly with a kids' microscope. Plant cells, crystals and fibres are much better for beginners.

How do I choose between a digital and an optical microscope?

Optical microscopes are good for practising traditional technique; digital microscopes have a screen, which makes it easier for several people to watch and to take photos. See our kids' microscope buying guide.
