for every age

Looking for more family activity ideas?

What shall we do today?

Board games are the easiest family activity to start: no going out, no screens, just one box that gets everyone around the table for 20 minutes. Even better, children practise taking turns, waiting, counting, strategy and dealing with winning and losing, skills that are hard to teach from a textbook.

In short: for ages 3–5, choose cooperative or hand–eye games with simple rules and games under 10 minutes; at 6–8, add matching, memory and simple strategy; from 9, try maths and multi-step strategy games.

4 signs of a good family game

Rules explained in 3 minutes

If a grown-up needs 20 minutes with the rulebook, the kids have already lost patience. Simple rules with room for variety work best.

Everyone has something to do

Long waits make little ones fidgety. Games with quick turns, or where everyone plays at once, are a better fit.

A balance of luck and skill

Young children need some luck so they don't always lose to adults; older children enjoy winning through strategy.

Room to level up

Good games let you change the rules as children grow, adding scoring, time limits or more cards, so one box lasts for years.

Turning game time into learning time

  1. Show first, explain later: play one demo round so children understand by watching.
  2. Think out loud: on your turn, say what you're thinking, like “I think they'll pick blue next, so I'll…”. Children copy this kind of strategic thinking.
  3. Make your child the referee: keeping score, dealing cards and checking rules builds responsibility and counting.
  4. Chat for a minute afterwards: “Which move made the difference?” “What will you do next time?” A one-minute review is great reflection practice.

What if my child is a sore loser?

Almost every child goes through a stage of crying, flipping the board or demanding a rematch when they lose. It's a normal part of development. Try this:

  • Agree before you start: “It's OK to feel upset if you lose, but we don't throw things.”
  • Play more cooperative games, where the family plays together against the game and nobody “loses”.
  • Model losing well: “Oh no, I lost! That's a shame. It was fun though. Rematch?”
  • Help your child name the feeling: “You feel really disappointed, don't you?” (Use the emotion faces chart.)
  • Don't let your child win every time. Losing sometimes is how they practise handling setbacks.

Start a family game night

A fixed 20–30 minute game time once a week works far better than playing now and then. Take turns choosing the game, and use the family game night scorecard to record each week's champion and a “good sport award”. Children will look forward to it every week.

FAQ

What age can children start board games?

From about two and a half to three, when children start to understand taking turns, they can play very simple matching or hand–eye games. Check for small parts, especially with under-3s.

We only have one child. Are board games still worthwhile?

Absolutely. Most children's games work with two players, and playing one to one makes it easier for parents to see how their child thinks.

How are board games different from tablet games?

Board games involve talking face to face, handling real feelings about winning and losing, and moving pieces with your hands, so there's more social and fine motor practice than with screen games.

Do maths board games really help with maths?

They give children a reason to do lots of mental maths, like multiplying to move further. They don't replace school lessons, but they build familiarity and confidence with numbers.
