10 kitchen science experiments
with things you already have

Baking soda, vinegar, sugar, lemons, the fridge: the kitchen is the best science lab. 10 kitchen experiments for ages 3–12, with materials, the science behind them, questions and safety tips.

What shall we do today?

No lab and no chemicals needed: your kitchen already has enough for real scientific investigation. Every experiment follows the same method: predict, then observe, then explain. That's exactly the inquiry skill Hong Kong's primary science subject focuses on.

Before you start: get a tray, a towel and a science experiment log. Do one experiment at a time and leave time for your child to ask “why?”.

How to do three more experiments

Red cabbage indicator

Chop red cabbage and soak it in hot water for 10 minutes (an adult handles the hot water), then strain it to get a purple liquid. Pour some into several clear cups and add vinegar, lemon juice, baking soda water and plain water. Watch the colours change: acids turn pinkish-red and alkalis turn blue-green. Ask your child to predict each colour first.

Pepper scatter

Pour water onto a plate and sprinkle a layer of pepper over it. Dab some washing-up liquid on a fingertip and touch the middle of the water. The pepper rushes to the edges, because the washing-up liquid lowers the water's surface tension.

Ice melting race

Take four ice cubes of the same size. Sprinkle one with salt, one with sugar, one with sand and leave one plain. Check every 5 minutes. It's a great way to practise a fair test: change only one thing and keep everything else the same.

4 questions that make experiments more meaningful

  • “What do you think will happen? Why?”
  • “Would it be different with another material?”
  • “How can we make the comparison fair?”
  • “Have you seen anything like this in everyday life?”

Has your child asked something you can't answer? Brilliant! Write it in the “Why?” question journal and find out together.

Safety tips

⚠️ Safety first: adults handle hot water, knives and the stove. Don't eat or drink experiment materials (unless stated otherwise). Wash hands afterwards. Use only a low-voltage LED with the lemon battery and never connect it to mains power.

FAQ

What age can children start doing experiments?

From age 3, children can join simple observation experiments like sink or float and melting ice. As they get older, add predicting, measuring and recording.

What if an experiment fails?

“Failure” is a result too. Work out why with your child: the amounts, the temperature, the order of steps? Adjust and try again, and that's a complete scientific investigation.

What tools do we need to look closely at crystals or cells?

A magnifying glass is enough for salt and sugar crystals. For finer structures, use a kids' microscope or a digital microscope.
