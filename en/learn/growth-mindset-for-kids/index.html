Growth mindset:
praise that beats the fear of failing

“You're so clever” can make children more afraid of failing. What a growth mindset is, how to praise effort and process, turning “I can't” into “I can't yet”, and using it in STEM play.

What shall we do today?

“Growth mindset” is an idea from American psychologist Carol Dweck: children who believe their abilities can grow through effort, strategies and practice are more willing to take on challenges and more likely to try again after failing. The things parents say every day shape how children see their own abilities.

In short: say “you're so clever” less, and “you tried lots of ways” more. Praising process, strategy and persistence teaches children that difficulty is part of learning.

Two mindsets compared

Fixed mindsetGrowth mindset
Facing a challengeAvoids it, afraid of failingGives it a go, finds it interesting
After a failure“I'm no good at this”“I need to try another way”
EffortNeeding effort means I'm not smartEffort is how I improve
When others succeedFeels threatenedLearns from how they did it

Swap the sentence: praise practice

❌ You're so clever!

✅ “You tried three different ways before you found the answer. That took patience!”

❌ You're a natural at drawing.

✅ “You used lots of colours this time and even added shadows. Very careful work.”

❌ Don't worry, it's easy!

✅ “This one is tricky. Let's take it step by step.”

❌ Never mind, it's not your thing.

✅ “You can't do it yet, and that means you can still learn.”

To make these a habit, print the growth mindset phrase cards and stick them on the fridge or by the desk.

The power of “yet”

When your child says “I can't do it”, add one word: “You can't do it yet.” This small change reminds them that not being able to do something now doesn't mean never. You can also look back together: “Remember when you couldn't ride a bike? What about now?”

STEM play is the perfect practice ground

Science experiments and engineering challenges are full of “failure”: paper bridges collapse, paper planes nosedive, results don't match predictions. Each one is a chance to practise a growth mindset:

  1. Say it upfront: “Scientists try many times before they succeed.”
  2. Record every attempt: use a science experiment log to note what you changed and what happened.
  3. Celebrate progress, not just results: “Your plane flew 2 steps further this time!”
  4. Share your own mistakes: let your child see that grown-ups get things wrong too, and work out what to do next.

FAQ

Should I never say my child is clever?

Saying it now and then is fine. The point is to describe more often what your child did and which strategies they used, so they understand that success comes from things they can control.

What should I say when my child tried hard and still failed?

Acknowledge the feeling first, then think it through together: “You worked really hard and it didn't turn out as you hoped. That must be disappointing. Which step could we change?” Focus on the next step, not just comfort.

What age is it suitable for?

You can start from the toddler years. Young children may not understand the idea, but they gradually absorb it from the way their parents talk.
